Hawaiʻi · 1975–2025 · official public data

Three crises,
one small state.

Hawaiʻi locks up fewer people than it did twenty years ago, and reported crime has fallen for four decades. Yet homelessness has climbed, and roughly 865 people from Hawaiʻi are still held 2,600 miles away on the continental United States. Something in the relationship between these three numbers does not add up — and that is the point of this page.

Every figure below comes from a published government dataset. Nothing is modelled, extrapolated, or guessed. Where the data cannot answer a question, this page says so.

The shape of the problem

Three measurements, three different stories

The intuition is that crime, imprisonment and homelessness are one problem wearing three masks — more crime means more prisoners, and more prisoners means more people released onto the street. Hawaiʻi's own data lets us test that intuition. It only partly holds.

Pillar one

Homelessness

Once a year, in January, volunteers across the islands count everyone sleeping in shelters and on the street. It is an imperfect census — but it is the same imperfect census every year, which makes the trend meaningful even when the level is not.

People counted homeless in Hawaiʻi, 2007–2025

HUD Point-in-Time counts, statewide. Toggle series in the legend.

Two years are not comparable

2021 and 2025 were sheltered-only counts — the unsheltered street count was not conducted, so those totals measure a different population than the other years. They are shaded on the chart and shown as a break in the line rather than a dip.

The 2024 jump needs caution

The count rose from 6,223 in 2023 to 11,637 in 2024 — a single-year increase of 87%. HUD's CoC-level file shows the increase concentrated almost entirely in the Hawaiʻi Balance of State CoC (2,195 → 7,140), which points to a change in how that region counted rather than a one-year doubling of homelessness. The number is published and real; the interpretation is not settled.

Update · the 2026 count, released after this data was compiled

Oʻahu's January 2026 Point-in-Time count found more than 4,500 people homeless — roughly flat overall, but a 20% fall in unsheltered people and a 35% rise in sheltered people since 2024. The composition changed sharply: homeless children rose 43% since 2022, and people aged 60+ (19% of the total) rose 45%.

This supports the caution in the chart above: part of the 2023→2024 statewide jump was a counting and shelter-capacity effect, not a sudden doubling of people on the street. It also shows what the aggregate hides — the crisis is ageing, and it is increasingly families. Source: Civil Beat, May 2026 ↗

Pillar two

Crime

Reported crime in Hawaiʻi has fallen substantially since the 1970s. That is the clearest long-run trend in this entire collection of data — and it is the one most often missing from public debate.

Index crime rate per 100,000 residents, 1975–2021

A stacked decomposition: the two bands sum to the total index crime rate (the dark outline). Property crime is the larger band, violent crime the thinner one on top — shown together so neither can hide behind the other's scale.

Violent crime, shown on its own scale

Per 100,000 residents. Plotting this against total crime flattens it into a straight line at the bottom — so it gets its own axis.

The counter-intuitive part

Violent crime has not fallen the way property crime has. The violent crime rate was 218 per 100,000 in 1975 and 267 in 2021 — a rise of about 22%, while the overall index fell 52%. “Crime is down” is true of theft; it is not true of violence.

By county, as rates

Index offenses per 100,000 residents, so the four counties are directly comparable despite Honolulu holding two-thirds of the state's population.

What crime actually consists of

Index offenses by type, 2021 — the last year of this series.

Proportion matters

Property crime outnumbers violent crime roughly 10 to 1. Murder is vanishingly rare — about 1.9 per 100,000. Policies aimed at “crime” in aggregate are mostly policies aimed at theft.

Every offense, on its own axis

Eight categories over 46 years. Each panel has its own vertical scale, because larceny runs about 20,000 a year and murder about 30 — on a shared axis the small categories would be a flat line at zero. Compare the shape of each panel, and read the % change next to each title. Values shown are for 2021.

What the individual categories show

The headline “crime is down 52%” averages three completely different stories. The figures below are per-capita, 1975→2021.

Falling sharply: burglary −82%, murder −76%, robbery −51%, larceny-theft −47%. Rising sharply: aggravated assault +163% and rape +38% — though the national rape definition changed in 2013, so treat that series cautiously. Arson begins only in 1980 (−49% since then).

Watch the units toggle. Switch to raw counts and several verdicts flip: motor vehicle theft falls 17% per capita but rises 39% in raw numbers, because the resident population grew 67% over the same period. Per-capita is the fairer comparison; raw counts are what a police department actually handles.

Where this series ends — and why it matters

On 1 January 2014 Hawaiʻi began transitioning to the FBI's National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS), which counts incidents and up to ten offenses per incident rather than one summary offense. The state completed the transition in 2021 and retired this series. Post-2021 figures now live only in the Hawaiʻi Crime Dashboard, which is a Power BI view with no bulk download and no API. Any 2021-vs-2022 comparison on this page would be invalid, so none is offered.

Pillar three

Incarceration

Hawaiʻi is unusual among US states: it runs a unified prison and jail system, so a single department holds people awaiting trial and people serving multi-decade sentences. It also exports a substantial share of its prisoners.

Total assigned population vs. people housed out of state

Fiscal years ending 30 June. “Assigned” counts everyone under correctional jurisdiction.

The out-of-state question

At its 2009 peak, Hawaiʻi housed 2,077 people in contracted mainland facilities — 34.6% of its entire prison population, the highest share of any US state at the time. That share is now 23%. Contract beds have shrunk, but the practice has not ended: 865 people remain in FY2025.

What the in-state population is made of

Annual average head count in the state's eight adult facilities, 2010–2025.

The pretrial finding

In FY2025 the largest single category in Hawaiʻi's facilities is people not yet sentenced — 1,041 of 2,868, or 36%. Jails are, more than anything else, places where legally innocent people wait.

The hard part

How the three problems connect

This is where most analyses overreach. The data can show that these problems move together and overlap in the same populations. It cannot, on its own, show that one causes another. Here is what the evidence does and does not support.

What the common denominator reveals

Over this decade the crime rate falls 26% and the incarceration rate falls 8%. The homeless rate rises 7% — and it peaked in 2016 at 555 per 100,000, exactly when crime was already well down from its 2010 level. There is no common trajectory here. That divergence is the strongest evidence on this page that homelessness is not simply a downstream effect of crime.

  • 01

    Crime and imprisonment moved together — then decoupled

    Both rose through the 1970s–90s and both fell sharply after about 2007. But the prison population fell before the steepest crime declines in some years and after them in others. A single shared driver — or policy choices like justice reinvestment — fits this pattern better than either one causing the other.

  • 02

    Homelessness did not follow crime down

    This is the most important negative finding on the page. If crime were the engine of homelessness, the 2010s should have produced a steady decline. Instead the count rose from 5,834 (2010) to 7,921 (2016) — a 36% increase — while the crime rate fell 11% over the same six years. Both cannot be driven by the same force. Housing cost, not crime, is the better-supported driver.

    Caveat added after reviewing the reporting: this holds for index crime only. Enforcement against homeless people, and methamphetamine, connect the two systems in ways the index series cannot record — see The connection the index numbers miss.

  • 03

    The real link is at the point of release

    In FY2025 Hawaiʻi granted 473 paroles, released 416 people on parole, and had 980 people under parole supervision — plus everyone who left custody by maxing out their sentence. A person leaving custody with no address is at high risk of both re-offending and becoming homeless. This is the best-documented mechanism connecting the prison and homeless systems, and it is a reentry problem, not a crime-rate problem.

  • 04

    Punishment falls hardest on Native Hawaiians

    The clearest official figure is from the Bureau of Justice Statistics: on 31 December 2023, 1,692 of the 3,942 people in Hawaiʻi's correctional system were Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander — about 43%. In the 2020 Census that same group was about 27% of the state's population (alone or in combination). The overrepresentation repeats in the homeless count, where Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders were 2,545 of 6,828 people in 2025 — about 37%.

    That 37% is an undercount. It uses HUD's single-race category. On Oʻahu in 2026 the share of people counted homeless who were Hawaiian or Pacific Islander rose to 63% once multiracial people were included — “race alone” reporting systematically understates this disparity.

  • 05

    Geography is a policy, not a fact

    Holding people 2,600 miles from their families makes successful reentry harder and reentry is the mechanism that links prison to homelessness. The out-of-state contract is therefore not a neutral cost-saving measure — it feeds directly into the third crisis.

What this page will not claim

No correlation coefficient is offered between these series. The measurement windows do not align (crime ends 2021, homelessness is a January snapshot, prison is a fiscal-year average), the counts use incompatible denominators, and three data points do not identify a causal direction. Anyone presenting a single tidy correlation across these three measures is almost certainly overfitting.

But absence of a correlation is not evidence of absence of a connection. The quantitative sections above cannot see quality-of-life enforcement, addiction, or institutional capacity — which is precisely why the reporting section exists, and why it is labelled as reporting rather than data.

What the data cannot see

The connection the index numbers miss

Everything above says crime rates and homelessness do not move together. That is a statement about index crime — the eight legacy categories the FBI measures. Reporting from Honolulu Civil Beat shows the two are connected anyway, through two channels the aggregate series structurally cannot capture: enforcement of survival behaviour, and methamphetamine.

1. The state polices survival, not just crime

And index crime does not count it.

In August 2026 the Honolulu City Council considered Bill 53, which would fine anyone possessing a shopping cart outside a store up to $2,000 or jail them for 30 days. A man interviewed in Iwilei said his cart “helps keep me upright” — it was functioning as a walker.

Charges like this — shopping carts, sit-lie, trespass, obstructing a sidewalk — are not index offences. They never appear in the crime chart above. A 52% fall in index crime is entirely compatible with rising enforcement against homeless people, because the two measure different things.

2. Methamphetamine is the connective tissue

One drug links property crime, arrest, prison and homelessness.

Hawaiʻi was the first place in the U.S. where smoked crystal meth took hold, and it is now the only state where meth kills more people than opioids. Civil Beat obtained autopsies for 100 meth-related deaths on Oʻahu in ten months across 2024–25.

This is the mechanism a correlation coefficient would miss entirely: the same addiction drives the burglary and car theft in the offence chart, the arrest, the prison bed, and the loss of housing — in that order, for the same people, repeatedly.

3. The delivery system failed, not just the funding

Which is why money has not moved the number.

The state auditor found $13.7 million in improper or unauthorised spending in the governor's flagship kauhale (tiny-home) programme. In one case, 20 tiny homes that cost taxpayers $716,000 were sold to a Kalihi church for $1, with support services dropped, while more than 30 elderly residents kept paying rent.

Honolulu's own auditor found the city's homeless services programme “too disorganized to assess.” A rising homeless count is not proof that policy failed for lack of money; it is partly proof that the money was not delivered.

4. The prison got smaller and more dangerous

A falling headcount is not a success measure.

Confirmed or suspected suicides accounted for eight of the fifteen deaths at Hālawa Correctional Facility over two years, and about one third of all deaths in the statewide correctional system in 2024–25. Nationally, suicides are about 8% of prison deaths.

The population chart above shows a 38% decline since 2005 — a genuinely good trend. It says nothing about the conditions inside, which is where Civil Beat's reporting finds the crisis has moved.

How this changes the argument above

The earlier finding still holds: crime rates do not drive homelessness, and the reentry pathway is the best-evidenced link in the administrative data. But “decoupled” was too strong a word. The correct statement is narrower:

Index crime rates and homelessness have diverged. The criminal justice system and homelessness have not. They are connected through the enforcement of quality-of-life offences and through methamphetamine — neither of which the index series measures.

A reader who took only the charts would conclude these are three separate problems. A reader of the reporting would conclude they are one population cycling through three systems. Both observations are true, and a site that showed only the first would be misleading.

Known gaps

  • The state open-data portal holds one crime dataset and effectively no corrections datasets.
  • Post-2021 crime data has no machine-readable release at all.
  • The latest published recidivism cohort is FY2016.
  • Maui County police statistics could not be retrieved; the county site blocks automated access.
  • No state-level victimization survey exists.
Structural break to keep in mind

On 1 January 2024 the Hawaiʻi Department of Public Safety was redesignated as the Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, and its law-enforcement divisions moved to a new Department of Law Enforcement. Even the 2025 Data Book still credits the abolished “Department of Public Safety,” which is a useful reminder to check the publisher, not just the table.
